# Results, in plain words

Everything below is about receivers covered one-on-one ("man coverage"). "More open than normal" means
they had more space than a receiver usually gets on that kind of play. 0 means an average amount of space.

- How many plays: we looked at 9,943 one-on-one routes run by 433 receivers; 85 of them were covered this way at least 40 times.
- Getting open leads to catches: when a receiver is about average, 52% of throws to him are caught. With one extra yard of space the catch rate climbs to 63%, and with two it reaches 72%. This pattern is very unlikely to be luck (based on 2052 throws).
- Catch rate by how open the receiver was: more covered than usual: 41% (207 throws), a bit covered: 41% (786 throws), a bit open: 61% (573 throws), more open than usual: 76% (486 throws)
- Yards gained, by how open the receiver was: more covered than usual: 5.2, a bit covered: 6.2, a bit open: 8.8, more open than usual: 10.2
- Quarterbacks throw to the more open receivers more often: least open: 13%, below average: 18%, above average: 23%, most open: 28%
- Is it a repeatable skill? A receiver's first-four-weeks number and his next-four-weeks number move together, but only loosely (across 97 players). So it's a real skill, with real week-to-week noise.
- Against different one-on-one looks (the league averages near 0 by design): Cover-0: caught 54% of 228 throws across 1061 routes, Cover-1: caught 55% of 1674 throws across 8103 routes, 2-Man: caught 59% of 150 throws across 779 routes
- The best at getting open: Mark Andrews (TE) +0.59, Henry Ruggs (WR) +0.45, Darren Waller (TE) +0.43, Elijah Moore (WR) +0.42, A.J. Brown (WR) +0.33
- A clear example: Mark Andrews had 5.6 yards of space, about 3.1 is normal (game 2021091913, play 1022)

## Getting open: how fast?

- What "getting open" means here: most receivers already stand a couple yards off the nearest defender at the snap because of where they line up, so that isn't beating anyone. We instead count the moment a receiver pulls two yards clear of the closest the defender had gotten to him.
- How many manage it: 11% of 9,943 one-on-one routes break clear before the throw, and it takes about 2.1 seconds on average.
- Getting clear draws the ball: receivers thrown to 24% of the time when they got clear quickly, 31% when they got clear later, and 20% when they never got clear.
- But breaking free earlier is not clearly better than later: when thrown to, catch rate was 72% (141 throws) for the quick ones vs 71% (171 throws) for the later ones. Honestly, the link between how fast a receiver gets open and whether he's thrown to is weak; the real story is that getting open at all draws the ball.

## Did the open man get the ball?

- On 2,052 one-on-one plays, the receiver who got the most open was NOT the one thrown to 69% of the time.
- Passing him up cost something: throws to the most open man were caught 68% of the time for 9.1 yards (636 throws); throws to someone else were caught 49% of the time for 7.2 yards (1416 throws).
- One fair caveat: we judge "most open" at the moment the ball is thrown, but the quarterback decides a little earlier, so some of these are timing and reads rather than clear mistakes.
